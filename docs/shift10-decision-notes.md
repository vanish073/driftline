# Shift 10 decision notes (working file; folded into DESIGN §19 at the end of the shift, then deleted)

- D81 the hot-account benchmark (§7.5, §17): `bench` `HotAccountBenchmark`, run by `scripts/hot-accounts.sh` in a fresh PostgreSQL 17.12 container (768 MB, `shared_buffers=128MB`, `fsync=on`, `synchronous_commit=on`, READ COMMITTED) with production's roles: Flyway migrates as the superuser, the benchmark posts as the DML login. One merchant's two hot accounts (gateway receivable, payments clearing) take every capture, each a distinct payment, from N concurrent writers (N ∈ {1, 4, 16, 64}, pool N + 2); after a 5 s warm-up, 20 s are measured: committed transactions per second, p50 and p99 commit latency of a ledger post, and p50 and p99 of a balance read taken every 50 ms alongside; under APPEND_SNAPSHOT the snapshotter (`PostgresStore.snapshotBalances`) runs every second. Each run uses a fresh merchant and ends by checking the balance equals the sum posted. A run during which the machine swapped in for 5 consecutive seconds is rejected (vmstat). Results (`docs/results/hot-accounts.csv`, `.txt`, `.svg`; laptop of §15.1): ROW_LOCK peaks near 280 tps at 16 writers and falls to about 200 at 64 with a p99 over a second, since every writer queues on the balance row; APPEND_SNAPSHOT scales to about 1,360 tps at 64 writers with a p99 near 150 ms, and pays at read time: balance-read p50 33 ms at 64 writers against ROW_LOCK's 3 ms. **Not done, stated:** nothing in production schedules the snapshotter. Nothing in production reads a balance either (only tests do; `driftline verify` uses SQL), so today the cost is nil, but a balance read under APPEND_SNAPSHOT grows with the entries since the last snapshot; a deployment that reads balances must run `snapshotBalances` periodically, as the benchmark does.
- D82 the load test (§17; REQUIREMENTS "events per second sustained on your laptop, and p99 time from event to match"): `bench` `LoadGenerator` is an open-model generator: one scheduler thread fires each arrival at its slot (`start + k / rate`), whatever the responses do, over the JDK HTTP client's async sends, so a slow system cannot slow the arrivals and hide its latency; at most 1,024 requests are in flight and an arrival beyond that is not sent and counts as skipped. Each order is three events through the real ingest API in arrival order: the merchant's `order.created` (bearer token, Idempotency-Key), the gateway's signed `payment.succeeded` (with a fake customer contact in the sandbox's pattern, so the vault's encrypt-and-store path is part of the load and `scripts/pii-scan.sh` still checks for leaks), and `order.paid`; merchants in turn; event times of now, truncated to milliseconds (the codecs refuse finer ones; the first smoke run was 1,198 of 1,200 requests refused for that, and a unit test now covers it); the first three refusal bodies are kept in the step's JSON. `scripts/load-test.sh` runs it against the Compose `core` profile (every role in one JVM with production's 256 MB heap cap) plus Prometheus alone, with Gradle stopped and 12 merchants whose first settlement date is two days ahead (so no settlement alert fires mid-run; `BOOTSTRAP_DAYS_BACK`); it steps the offered rate up (25, 50, 100, 200, 400 events/s, 10 minutes each) and stops at the first step that is not sustained. **Sustained** (`scripts/load-evaluate.py`), over a step's last 5 minutes: the least-squares slope of `sum(driftline_consumer_lag_records)` at most 0.1 records/s, and the generator saw no refusal, transport error, skip or unanswered request, and the machine did not swap in for 5 consecutive seconds during the step (vmstat with an epoch per line; §15.1); a step during which the machine paused (a gap of more than 5 s between vmstat's once-a-second samples, or samples not covering the step) is INVALID, since it measured the pause: the first real run slept through its first step (gaps of 575 s and 29,798 s, a laptop asleep or a suspended WSL VM), came back to timeouts, a PostgreSQL restart and swapping, and its files were discarded. Then 10-minute steps at 50% and 80% of the highest sustained rate give the p50 and p99 event-to-match (`histogram_quantile` over the last 5 minutes). Records: `load-step-<rate>.json` (the generator's counts, the lag series and slope, events consumed and ingest accepted per second, event-to-match p50 and p99, swap), `load-p99-50pct.json`, `load-p99-80pct.json`, `load-summary.json`, `load-test.txt`. The step granularity bounds the answer: the true limit lies between the highest sustained step and the next. Event-to-match here measures every applied event, as D78 states. Two script bugs the smoke runs caught: the exit handler restored a failed status under `set -e` and so aborted before the teardown (it now sets `+e`), and the core teardown did not remove Prometheus (the handler removes it). Each step's record also carries, for information only (no verdict depends on them), the JVM's GC pauses and CPU share over the window and the machine's busiest second (`jvm`, `machine.window_*`), which tell a latency tail caused by the machine from one caused by Driftline. **The second real run (2026-11-22) was stopped and its files discarded**: 25 events/s sustained, with a p99 of 2.1 s that came from one 20-second CPU surge on the machine (run queue 35, 5% idle) during which a young-generation GC pause reached 2.69 s while Driftline itself used at most about 1.4 of 12 cores; 50 events/s sustained, p99 0.16 s; 100 events/s judged sustained with a p50 of 1.25 s and a p99 of 13.8 s, while the lag gauge peaked at 49. During the 200 events/s step the backlog measured by hand (the topic's end offsets minus `partition_leases.next_offset`) grew from 10,516 to 23,225 in 95 s and no partition's stored offset moved for over 20 s (Kafka up to 230% CPU near its 640 MB limit, Driftline at 463 of 512 MB); the lag gauge was not read during that step, so what the old rule would have said there is unknown. The gauge was updated only by the stalled loop, so the run stopped to fix the measurement first (D83). **The third run (2026-11-22, with D83) was stopped too**: 25 events/s sustained (backlog at most 1 over 21 samples; p50 13 ms, p99 1.18 s, the tail matching a 1.75 s full collection, `MarkSweepCompact`, while the machine had 9% idle CPU); 50 events/s was then judged not sustained only because the swap rule fired, on 4 to 16 KB swapped in each second for 6 seconds (960 KB over the 10 minutes), while Driftline had its best step (backlog at most 6, p99 0.15 s, longest GC pause 0.05 s). That exposed two faults in the rule, both fixed after seeing this result, which is stated here: (1) any swap-in counted, so one page a second, which cannot distort a measurement, failed a step; a second now counts only if it swaps in at least 256 KB (64 pages, enough page faults to cost the JVM a visible share of its time), still for 5 consecutive seconds, and the total is recorded either way. (2) A step whose machine paused or swapped was judged *not sustained*, which named the previous rate as Driftline's limit; such a step is now INVALID (exit 2), stepping stops, and `load-summary.json` says by `stopped_by` whether the limit lies between two steps (`not_sustained`), was not found (`invalid_step`: the highest sustained rate is only a lower bound) or is above every rate tried. **The fifth run (2026-11-23, with D83 and D84) is the published one** (`docs/results/load-*.json`, `load-test.txt`): 25 events/s sustained (event-to-match p50 15 ms, p99 46 ms); 50 events/s sustained (p50 28 ms, p99 1.5 s; longest GC pause 0.18 s; backlog at most 37 while the client's estimate showed 0); 100 events/s not sustained (the backlog rose to 2,889 two-thirds into the window and drained by its end, a slope of 3.1 records/s; p99 38 s; every request accepted; the client's estimate peaked at 180, a sixteenth of the backlog); so the limit on this laptop lies between 50 and 100 events/s. At 50% and 80% of 50 events/s: 25 events/s, p50 17 ms and p99 2.7 s; 40 events/s, p50 26 ms and p99 2.0 s. The same 25 events/s gave a p99 of 46 ms as the first step and 2.7 s right after the overloaded 100 events/s step, with the same p50: the tail on this laptop varies between runs of the same rate by that much, and the README reports both rather than the better one. At the 100 events/s step's busiest second, 11.3 of 12 cores were busy: the stack's containers about 5.5 (Driftline 2.6, PostgreSQL 1.8, Kafka 1.1), the generator 0.3 on average, and the rest outside both (Docker, WSL, Windows).
- D83 the matcher's backlog, measured from outside its loop (§13, §17): `driftline_consumer_lag_records{partition}` came from `consumer.currentLag()`, which only the partition driver's loop reported, once per poll; a matcher stuck inside a chunk (the load test saw one stall for over 20 s) froze the gauge at its last value, which blinded both the `ConsumerLagGrowing` alert and the load test's sustained rule. Now `BacklogProbe` (matcher role) measures it every 5 s on its own thread: for each partition this instance owns, the topic's end offset (its own Kafka admin client; the driver's consumer is not thread-safe) minus the offset stored as processed (`PartitionRepo.nextOffset`, committed in the same transaction as the records' effects, so the Store port is unchanged), never below 0; a partition no longer owned drops out and its new owner reports it, so a sum over instances counts each partition once; a failed read is logged and the previous values stay. The metric name is unchanged, so the alert, its rule tests and the Grafana board need no change; the client's estimate stays as `driftline_consumer_client_lag_records`, and the load test records both, so the README can say whether the old gauge under-reported. `PartitionDriverIT` stalls a driver at CP7 with real Kafka and PostgreSQL, produces 36 more records, and checks that the probe counts all 36 while the client's estimate does not, then that the backlog drains. Two defects found on the way: (1) `MultiGauge.register(rows, true)` removes and registers every row again on each refresh, so a scrape in between could miss a series whose value had changed (the likely cause of `ObservabilityIT` missing `driftline_deadline_headroom_seconds` once in four runs; that miss was not reproduced); `GaugeRows` keeps a row's meter and changes only its value, and `BookMetrics` and the probe use it. (2) A time bomb: `ObservabilityIT`, `SecretRotationIT` and `CustomerVaultIT` bootstrapped a merchant whose first settlement date was the literal 2026-11-18; once that date passed, `ObservabilityIT` failed on a clean checkout (its spans showed a second break, raised with the first record and escalated at once, whose call the test took for the expected one); they now use today plus two days. Neither is a simulator bug, so neither has a BUGS.md row.
- D84 Driftline's heap is committed from the start (§15.1): `-Xms256m -Xmx256m` in the Compose `x-app-env` block and the Kubernetes base ConfigMap (Kafka already ran with `-Xms` equal to `-Xmx`). The fourth load run (2026-11-23, with D83) sustained 25 events/s (event-to-match p50 12 ms, p99 46 ms) and failed 50 events/s: one full collection (`MarkSweepCompact`, cause "Allocation Failure") stopped the JVM for 21.8 s, so 2,116 requests timed out at the generator's 10 s, Prometheus missed a scrape and the backlog reached about 600 before draining. With no `-Xms` the heap had started small (old generation 62 MB committed, 41 MB used; eden 25 MB) and SerialGC grows it only through full collections: a young collection needs the old generation's free space to cover what may survive it, so a burst forced a full one instead, after which the heap grew to 138 MB old and 55 MB eden. Committing the heap removes that trigger; the cap, and so the most the heap can use, is unchanged, so the containers' memory limits need no change. It does not explain why that collection took 21.8 s rather than well under a second: the whole machine was saturated at the time (3 to 8% idle CPU, 24 to 33 runnable threads, about 40% in the kernel) while Driftline's JVM used about 4 of the 12 cores, and the run recorded nothing per container. So `scripts/load-test.sh` now also samples `docker stats` every few seconds (`load-dockerstats.txt`), the generator reports its own CPU time, and each step's record gives every container's mean and peak CPU over the window and at the sample nearest the machine's busiest second, beside the machine's busy cores then; what none of them accounts for is outside the stack and the generator (Docker itself, WSL, Windows). The fourth run's results are superseded by a run on this configuration rather than published.
